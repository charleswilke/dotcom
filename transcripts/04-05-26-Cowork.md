# Befuddled Discomfort — Working Conversation

*A collaborative exchange between Charles Wilke and Claude, April 5, 2026*

---

**Charles:**

Is this the level we've reduced our simmering ire?

Some time ago I wrote about the fading moment when folks should cherish their confident ire. The time when you could confidently point and laugh. Definitively classify. That *this specific thing* was made with AI. Silly computer, and silly person for trying to pass this off as your own.

But then, as things tend to do, the technology improved.

Will Smith eating spaghetti is my favorite benchmark. Rather, I should say it *was*. Now, it just looks like Will Smith agreed to shoot a silly little video leaning into a meme from 2023. But we're still in the first third of 2026, and the idea of Will Smith's grainy contorted face morphing through bastardized clumps of pixellated noodles, is quaint. Almost nostalgic. That was like, 20 months ago.

In baby terms, we still gauge time in months when it's less than two years.

Now it's laughable to think we could discern any spaghetti footage, any celebrity footage, any war footage. The tail wagged the dog. The tail is the dog. There is no spoon.

What do we really want from creation? Or is the satiation of consuming bespoke hallucinations really all that was wanted? We just want it to taste good, tickle an emotion, and leave without lingering.

I think we're simpler folk than we dare let on. We recoil and mock, then we accept and marvel. It happened with computer graphics, CG effects, digital replacement backgrounds, LED volumes, and motion tracked makeup and costumes. 3D printed baubles were laughed out of craft fairs. Now, you can build a car at home with enough gumption and pocket change.

The rubicon wizzed by. Silently. Under the guffaws of mockery. Past the gathered eyes squinted in ridicule.

You don't know what you're fighting. Maybe it's time to look inward.

Who you fightin'?

---

**Claude:**

This is a good piece of gristle to chew on.

What strikes me is the sleight of hand in the middle — you shift from "we can't tell anymore" to "maybe we never really cared about telling." That's the sharper cut. The spaghetti benchmark isn't just about fidelity improving. It's about the audience revealing that fidelity was never the actual currency. The currency was always the feeling. The craft was just the tollbooth.

And I think you're right that the mockery phase is mostly self-protective. "Silly computer" is really "I still matter, right?" The guffaws aren't directed at the technology. They're directed at the implications. If the thing can do the thing, then what was the thing? Was it ever about the doing, or was it always about the having-done? The identity of being a maker.

But here's where I want to push back a little, or at least push sideways: "Who you fightin'?" is a great closer, but I think there's a version of this that's too clean. Too neat a bow. Because some of the people fighting *do* know what they're fighting. They're fighting the dissolution of provenance as a meaningful concept. Not "is this good" but "did someone mean this." And that's not nothing. A forgery of a Rothko might move you to tears, and the tears are real, but something does change when you learn. Not for everyone. But for enough people that it's worth interrogating rather than dismissing as cope.

Your 3D printing analogy is apt but also revealing in a way I'm not sure you intended — those printers *did* get accepted at craft fairs, but they also split the craft fair world in two. There are now explicitly handmade markets. The acceptance didn't dissolve the distinction, it formalized it. Made it a genre choice rather than a default.

So maybe the rubicon didn't whiz by silently. Maybe it just didn't look like a river. Maybe it looked like a fork in the road, and most people took the wider path without noticing it was a choice.

The question I'd throw back: is "we're simpler folk than we dare let on" a confession or an absolution? Because it reads like both, and those two things point in very different directions.

**Charles:**

Damn, I suppose you're right. I guess it's the process of elbowing for room on the narrower path.

---

**Claude:**

Right. And there's something worth sitting with there — the narrower path isn't narrow because fewer people want it. It's narrow because it now requires *declaration*. Before, you were just making things. Now you're making things *by hand*, and the "by hand" part is load-bearing. It's a modifier where there used to be no need for one.

That's the real indignity, I think. Not that the machines got good. But that the humans who do the thing now have to *explain* that they do the thing. The craft became its own adjective. And elbowing for room on that path means constantly answering a question nobody used to ask: why this way?

And "because I meant it" is a real answer, but it's an exhausting one to keep giving. Especially when the wider path doesn't require any justification at all. Nobody asks why you used the easy thing. The default is invisible. Only the deviation needs a reason.

Which maybe circles back to your "simpler folk" line. The wider path wins not because people are shallow but because *not choosing* is always easier than choosing. The narrower path demands you keep choosing it, every day, against friction. That's not simplicity — that's thermodynamics. Energy flows downhill. The fork didn't make people simpler. It just made the simple option available.

So the elbowing — that's not fighting the technology. That's fighting entropy. And you don't win that fight. You just keep showing up to it.

---

**Charles:**

But in that regard, it polishes the surface, increases the shine.

---

**Claude:**

Yeah. That's the thing nobody on the wider path gets. The friction isn't a cost — it's the finish. The resistance is what gives the surface something to catch light.

Which is almost annoyingly literal when you think about craft. A hand-thrown pot has tooth to it. The lathe marks, the slight asymmetry, the place where the thumb pressed too hard — that's not a defect the potter failed to eliminate. That's the record of a negotiation between intention and material. Sand it smooth and you lose the story. The imperfection *is* the polish.

And the wider path can simulate that now, of course. You can prompt for "slight imperfections, handmade feel." But that's decoration, not history. It's the difference between a scar and a tattoo of a scar.

So maybe the narrower path doesn't just get narrower — it gets *higher resolution*. The people still on it become more attuned to what they're actually doing and why. The elbowing forces an articulation that the unchallenged maker never needed. And articulation sharpens intent. You start making things *harder on purpose* and discovering that the hardness was where the meaning lived all along.

Which is a weird gift from the machines, if you're willing to accept it from that direction. They didn't devalue the craft. They burned away everything that *wasn't* the craft and left the core standing there, obvious, maybe for the first time.

**Charles:**

That's very interesting, yeah, it felt like fandom absorbed craft for a while there.

---

**Claude:**

That's a really precise way to put it. Fandom *did* absorb craft. The consumption apparatus got so good at celebrating the *output* that it swallowed the process whole. You didn't need to understand how a thing was made to have a sophisticated opinion about it. Taste became the skill. Curation became the craft.

And that worked for a while because there was still a bottleneck — someone, somewhere, had to actually make the thing. Fandom could orbit the output all it wanted, but the maker was still at the center, even if nobody was looking at them. Gravity still pointed inward.

But generative tools dissolved that bottleneck, and suddenly fandom didn't need a center anymore. You could go from taste to artifact without passing through craft at all. The orbit became the thing. And for a lot of people that felt like liberation — *I can finally make the thing I could always see in my head.* Which is real. That's a real feeling.

But what got lost in that collapse is something almost impossible to explain to someone who hasn't felt it: the resistance *changing you*. Not you shaping the material — the material shaping you back. The pot pushes against the potter's hands and the potter becomes someone slightly different by the end. That feedback loop between maker and medium is where the actual transformation happens. The artifact is almost a byproduct.

So when fandom absorbed craft, it kept the byproduct and discarded the transformation. Which looks like a clean trade from the outside. You still get the pot. But nobody's hands changed.
