In [*Communion*](https://charleswilke.com/read/communion), he reportedly says he’d be fine without Doc. Then you describe him waiting for his brother to come home. There’s that distance between declared independence and visible attachment.

And then *Vulnerable* arrives with the subtitle [*Posturing while exposed*](https://charleswilke.com/read/vulnerable). Guard, shield, block. We learn to announce our invulnerability while our actual lives keep revealing what matters to us. You lose your job, get your friends together, and make something that gives the world another way to hurt you—and another way to meet you.

My read of the thread: **caring makes an opening in us.** Both essays explore what becomes possible when we let that opening stay open. Giving an unfamiliar mind consideration. Letting someone encounter something we’ve made. Neither comes with assurance that we’ll be understood.

For a song, I keep coming back to that mismatch between what the mouth declares and what the body does. Saying you’re fine. Listening for footsteps anyway. There’s affection, pride, fear, and a little humor in that.

A small seed:

I told you I’d be fine  
And I meant it when I swore  
But I still know your footsteps  
From this side of the door

**“From This Side of the Door”** could hold a lot: the dog, the artist, the person trying to reach somebody whose interior world they can never fully know.

Is that near the thread you’re feeling, or are you hearing something else between them?

# Finding the first sound

## Charles

What kind of sound are you hearing for this? I’m thinking something intimate that opens up as the song goes on, but I'm wondering what genre would feel best? Maybe like a Bob Seger or Bruce Springsteen, like fire in the belly, light and times heavy at others, soulful without feeling too self-important

## Codex

I hear **soulful heartland rock**—acoustic guitar, piano, organ, and a rhythm section that gradually puts some weight behind the voice. There’s an ache in it, but also warmth and a pulse.

From your Seger reference, I’d take the vocal grain and that feeling of someone finally getting something off their chest. From Springsteen, the attention to ordinary details and the conviction that this small domestic moment deserves a whole song. **The fire comes from how much the singer cares, even while trying to sound casual.**

I’d try **around 84 BPM, in 4/4**, with a relaxed backbeat. Enough space to tell the story, enough movement that it doesn’t sink into sorrow.

Here’s how I hear it growing:

- **First verse:** Close vocal and acoustic guitar. A little room around them. “Don’t worry about me” gets an almost amused delivery—the singer knows this routine.
- **First chorus:** Bass enters, with a few piano notes. The melody opens on “fine,” then comes down into the admission about the footsteps.
- **Second and third verses:** Drums settle in, and a Hammond organ starts filling the spaces. A clean electric guitar answers the vocal in short phrases. The notebook verse should have a little affection and embarrassment in it.
- **Bridge:** The band pulls back as the singer nearly puts the notebook away. Then “Got a minute? Listen to this” becomes the invitation for everyone to come in.
- **Final chorus:** Full drums, ringing guitar, more chest in the vocal. Maybe one rough harmony joins on “I still know your footsteps.” You can feel the effort of saying it.
- **Outro:** Back close again, with a little organ lingering beneath the dog settling on the floor.

I’d keep some breath and little imperfections in the performance. “Nothing. Not a lot” can practically be spoken. By the last chorus, the same person has found the nerve to sing out.

The lift should feel like **relief at finally being heard**. That gives it the soul and the fire while keeping its feet in that kitchen.

## Codex

The chorus already has a strong singing shape. **“Fine,” “swore,” and “door” give the voice somewhere to open up and hold.** I’d give each line two bars at that relaxed tempo, leaving space around the admissions.

The verses can move more freely. Conversational singing works through uneven line lengths; the useful test is whether the important words land comfortably, and whether the singer gets to breathe.

Several of your edits help:

- **“Don’t you worry about me”** has personality. Let “Said, don’t you” act as a pickup into **WOR**ry. It can sound casually reassuring while revealing a practiced defense.
- **“Then the corner ate the car”** is vivid and easy to sing. “Ate” and “car” both give the voice room.
- **“I can croon them to the dog”** adds warmth. That long vowel in “croon” almost invites a little playful delivery.

I hear four places worth adjusting:

**“Till the room had gotten dark.”**  
“Had gotten” takes up space without giving the singer much feeling to work with. I’d try **“Till the room went dark.”** Hold “dark,” or let the guitar finish the phrase. A shorter line can make the waiting more palpable.

**“With a coffee ring soaking thru the page.”**  
This is singable, but “coffee ring soaking” packs several substantial words together. **“With coffee soaking through the page”** keeps your active image and moves more easily:

“With **COF**fee **SOAK**ing through the **PAGE**.”

**“I say, ‘Nothing.’ lost in the fog.”**  
Rhythmically, this works. My hesitation is about what we see: the notebook, kettle, and dog are wonderfully specific, while “the fog” moves into abstraction. For the almost spoken delivery we discussed, I hear **“I say, ‘Nothing,’ laugh it off.”** We can hear the deflection happen. If that fog is an important feeling for you, though, there’s room to keep it.

**“Then I let you tell your day.”**  
This is the line from my draft that most needs another pass. “Tell your day” is awkward to say naturally, which will follow it into the singing. I’d try **“Then I shut up for a change.”** It has a little self-directed humor, lands firmly, and makes the act of listening concrete. “Change” gives us a loose rhyme with “day” and “say.”

I’d also leave a small breath after **“I lost my place a little.”** Then “When you asked to hear it again” can arrive with an audible smile. That moment deserves some ease.

My strongest instinct is to **let the verse endings breathe**. The singer is gradually finding the nerve to be heard; those little gaps can carry as much of that story as the words.

# A darker sound and the final lyric

## Charles

hmm, I'm thinking I want to shift the style a bit. It's feeling a little too Wal-mart country for my taste, maybe a little dash of leonard cohen? like a deep soulful singer, and let's get more creative with the instrumentation, could be interesting what kind of soundscapes we could imagine

## Codex

I’d move toward **dark chamber folk with a slow soul groove**. For the Cohen influence, I’d borrow vocal gravity, patience, and that little glint of humor. A deep voice that can make “Then I shut up for a change” land with a half-smile.

The interesting soundscape could come from **the house becoming musical around the singer**: kettle hiss stretched into a sustained breath, fingertips tapping wood, a metallic sound softened into a distant bell. A harmonium brings that wheezing, living quality; bass clarinet can answer the vocal with something almost conversational.

For the final lift, imagine bowed cello rising underneath and two higher voices answering the low lead. The room gradually feels inhabited. The fire lives in the friction between that grounded voice and the voices reaching above it.

Here’s a revised style brief, exactly 1,000 characters:

Dark chamber folk with a slow soul groove, 80 BPM, 4/4. Deep, intimate baritone with warm grain, conversational phrasing, dry humor and restrained intensity. The voice sits close and clear, opening into melody on the chorus. Start with a sparse, repeating nylon-string guitar figure and the breath of a gently wavering harmonium. Add round bass and a loping rhythm made from a soft kick, brushed snare and fingertips on wood. Bass clarinet answers the singer in short, searching phrases. Let a bowed cello gradually emerge beneath the verses. Turn household textures into faint musical atmosphere: kettle hiss stretched into air, a small metallic tap softened into a distant bell. At the bridge, expose the voice over a low sustained hum. Build the final chorus through widening harmonies, deeper bass and two high, imperfect voices answering the hook. Keep the feeling tender, earthy, wry and quietly unsettled. End with close voice, one plucked string and the harmonium breathing out into the room.
