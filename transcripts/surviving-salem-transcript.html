We are, underneath it all, creatures of habit. For all the scorn placed upon AI, much of the destination afforded its users resemble mass market appeal. The irony is that the easy tool leads to the well-trod path.

The argument against AI, especially in creative practice, continues to shift.

Early on, the theft argument held most of the weight. The fact that deviant art and getty image watermarks repeatedly appeared on generations. Seemed to pull full aspects of creation into new works. That certainly felt like theft.

But, if you think about it: If you grew up with every image you ever saw with a getty images watermark, you’d rightly assume all images have that same watermark. Our familiarity with form comes from the form we chose to become familiar with. In AI terms it’s the corpus it was trained upon. In human terms… it’s the corpus we were trained upon.

It’s interesting, the concept of AI’s theft being the fact it trained upon other work was loudest when the outputs looked awful. As it got better, the discussion shifted.

I’ve even seen people look back fondly on the wretched flowing mess that generated from DALL-E 2. Back when every prompt yielded four square fever dreams.

So now that the tells are harder to spot, we get a fun new type of McCarthyism, a salem witch trials of who’s who shouting over one another. Those who create with AI and focus on transparency are no longer a supple target for scorn. Now it’s those who we don’t like that deny they use it. Surely, they must.

The tells are gonna shift. Output speed, corpus availability. This person made this book in a weekend! Not possible!

But this shit happens. Kevin Williamson famously wrote the script for the first Scream movie in under a week. Like, a long weekend. So, not impossible, but the trained had that leg up in the past. Now, it’s just the fidelity and durability of the idea. The things we’ve knocked around in our melons that haven’t found a home til now.

And it’s so sad that this outlet gets shunned by those perched above the wall of creativity. The cost of craft. The sunken cost of education. There’s so much woven into this feeling. This fear, this scorn, this condemnation.

Codex

Saturday morning indeed. There’s a real essay in here—angry, curious, and alive. The central insight is strong: as AI’s obvious aesthetic failures disappear, opposition migrates from judging the work to policing the creator. The argument becomes less “this is bad” and more “prove you’re innocent.” That’s sharp, timely, and worth pursuing.

My favorite question is:

What is AI truly taking from you?

That’s the molten center of the piece. Income? Status? Scarcity? Identity? The moral authority conferred by years of training? The belief that creative ability distinguishes certain people from everyone else? “The allowable levels of abstraction between ideation and creation” is especially interesting. We’ve always accepted tools that remove steps—but apparently there’s an invisible point at which assistance becomes illegitimate authorship. Who gets to locate that point?

A few places where I’d push harder:

  • The essay currently treats the training-data objection as though it mostly receded because the results improved. That’s rhetorically tasty, but vulnerable. Consent, compensation, attribution, and corporate extraction remain substantive concerns even when the output is beautiful. Acknowledging that would strengthen your argument: those questions remain unresolved, but public behavior has increasingly shifted from structural criticism to personal suspicion.
  • The Salem/McCarthyism comparison brings heat, but it may be too historically enormous for the behavior you’re describing. Internet purity policing, false accusation, coerced confession, and contamination-by-association absolutely fit. “Witch hunt” can work if you make the mechanism precise. Otherwise readers may fight the analogy instead of encountering the point.
  • There’s a fascinating contradiction hiding in “the apparatus.” Generative AI may weaken publishers, studios, agencies, and credentialed creative classes—but the tools themselves belong to another apparatus: huge companies, proprietary models, rented infrastructure, opaque rules. Creation may be democratized while its means are newly centralized. That complication doesn’t undermine your case; it makes the essay much more formidable.
  • “Made quickly” and “made with AI” are increasingly being collapsed into the same accusation. Your Kevin Williamson example is useful because it reveals the old hierarchy: speed was admirable when attributed to talent, experience, inspiration, or genius. Now speed is treated as forensic evidence of fraud. That deserves another turn of the screw.
  • I’d be careful with “the trained had that leg up in the past.” Training still matters enormously; AI changes where it matters. Selection, judgment, revision, coherence, and durability become more important as execution becomes cheaper. You’re not arguing that craft vanished—you’re arguing that craft migrated.

Structurally, I think the piece wants to move through four clean stages:

  1. We distrust the unfamiliar output.
  2. Once the output becomes convincing, we distrust the person.
  3. That suspicion exposes anxieties about labor, status, and creative legitimacy.
  4. Beneath the supposed democratization lies a harder question: which forms of mediation count as authorship, and who benefits from deciding?

A few sentence-level notes:

  • “much of the destination afforded its users resemble” is tangled. Perhaps: “many of the destinations it offers lead straight back to mass-market appeal.”
  • “deviant art” should be “DeviantArt”; “Getty Images.”
  • “The fact that …” and “Seemed to pull …” are fragments that want joining.
  • “supple target” sounds bodily and slightly erotic. “Easy target,” “pliant target,” or “convenient target” would land more cleanly.
  • “sunken cost” should usually be “sunk cost.”
  • “trojan horse” → “Trojan horse.”
  • “Super heroes” → “superheroes.”
  • “til” → “’til,” though I’d probably use “until.”
  • Boldface throughout suppresses emphasis because everything arrives at the same volume.

I also think you should preserve this lovely line:

The things we’ve knocked around in our melons that haven’t found a home ’til now.

It has warmth and humanity that the more prosecutorial sections need.

The ending is intellectually good, but it disperses into several questions. I’d finish by converting one into a blade. Something in the neighborhood of:

Perhaps the argument was never only about whether machines can create. Perhaps it is about how much distance we will permit between an idea and its expression—and who loses authority when that distance collapses.

Or, more confrontationally:

If a feeling reached you before you knew how it was made, what exactly changed when you learned which tools carried it there?

That, to me, is your ending. It forces the reader to examine the moment aesthetic experience gets retroactively contaminated by ideology.

My overall verdict: don’t soften this. Complicate it. The rage is earned energy; the essay becomes exceptional when it also admits that AI is simultaneously a liberating abstraction, a corporate extraction machine, a threat to livelihoods, and a creative prosthesis. The contradiction is the subject.

Surviving Salem

Vilify and Deny

I pasted the first draft of this essay into an AI chat, and somewhere between Google Docs, the clipboard, and the machine, every paragraph became bold.

I hadn’t asked for that. But the machine received it as intention. The reader did too. Before we had even begun discussing authorship, the apparatus had already revised me.

It was probably a dumb formatting conversion. Yet the result still invited interpretation. Personality was attributed to an accident of infrastructure.

The machinery lies underneath.

We are, underneath it all, creatures of habit. We grow comfortable with certain kinds of mediation and suspicious of others. Autocorrect obscures imperfect spelling. An editor obscures weaknesses in prose. Green screen obscures the absence of a location. A teleprompter obscures memorization. Auto-Tune obscures pitch correction. Cinema itself obscures discontinuity through cuts, lenses, lighting, sets, Foley, compositing, and color grading.

“In camera” sounds pure only because we have culturally agreed that one collection of interventions counts as legitimate craft.

Every creative tool conceals something. We only call it deception until we’ve had enough time to call it craft.

The argument against AI, especially in creative practice, continues to shift.

Early on, the theft argument held most of the weight. DeviantArt and Getty Images watermarks appeared in generations. Sometimes whole aspects of existing work seemed to be pulled into something supposedly new. That certainly felt like theft.

The questions of consent, compensation, attribution, and corporate extraction remain unresolved. Better output does not make those questions disappear. But something interesting happened as the output improved: much of the public conversation moved away from structural criticism and toward personal suspicion.

When the tools produced obvious failures, the source material seemed to show through the cracks. If you grew up seeing every image with a Getty Images watermark, you might reasonably conclude that the watermark was simply part of what an image is. Our familiarity with form comes from the forms we encounter. In AI terms, it’s the corpus the model was trained upon. In human terms, it’s the corpus we were trained upon.

That doesn’t make the two processes ethically or mechanically identical. It does make the distinction more complicated than we often allow.

It’s strange that the theft argument was loudest when the outputs looked awful. As the tools got better and the obvious evidence became harder to spot, the discussion shifted.

I’ve even seen people look fondly upon the wretched, flowing messes generated by DALL-E 2, back when every prompt yielded four square fever dreams. Those images were grotesque, but they were safely grotesque. The machine announced itself. There was no danger of confusing its work with ours.

Now the tells are harder to spot, so we have found new ones.

Too many fingers. Suspicious punctuation. A certain lighting style. An adjective someone doesn’t usually use. Output speed. Corpus availability. This person made a book in a weekend. Not possible. Burn the witch.

We are entering a new kind of creative McCarthyism, a Salem of overlapping accusations in which the work itself is no longer sufficient evidence. Admit that you used AI and the admission disqualifies you. Deny that you used it and the denial becomes evidence that you must have.

The target is no longer merely the person who openly uses AI. It is anyone whose output, speed, polish, or personality arouses suspicion. Increasingly, it is simply someone we already dislike.

But this shit happens.

Kevin Williamson famously wrote the first draft of Scream in a matter of days. Before AI, unusual speed could be interpreted as talent, inspiration, preparation, obsession, or genius. Now it is increasingly treated as forensic evidence of fraud.

The trained had a leg up in the past, and training still matters. What changes is where that training becomes visible. When execution gets cheaper, judgment becomes more important. Selection matters. Revision matters. Coherence matters. The fidelity and durability of the idea matter.

Craft doesn’t disappear. It migrates.

Sometimes the tool isn’t covering a lack of ability at all. A filmmaker might be perfectly capable of securing a location but choose compositing for cost, control, safety, or because the location does not exist. A writer might know how to spell a word and still accept autocorrect’s intervention. The abstraction does not necessarily replace competence. It relocates where competence is exercised.

AI presses harder on this nerve because it can abstract portions of execution that we have traditionally treated as evidence of the creator’s inner life.

We keep mistaking the visible expenditure of labor for proof of authorship.

If you struggled over every sentence, traveled to the real desert, painted every frame, and spent fifteen years learning the sanctioned technique, then the result feels earned. Remove enough struggle and people begin to suspect that the expression itself is counterfeit.

That’s where so much of the fear, scorn, and condemnation gets woven together: the cost of craft, the sunk cost of education, the meal ticket, the recognition, the scarcity of opportunity, the quest for fame and notoriety. People have spent their lives climbing the wall around creativity. Now they can see others passing through doors that weren’t there before.

That doesn’t mean everyone walking through those doors will make something good. For all the infinite possibility promised by AI, many of the destinations it offers lead straight back to mass-market appeal. The easy tool often leads to the well-trodden path. Models have their corpus, users have their habits, and both exert a gravitational pull toward familiarity.

Democratizing creation does not democratize taste, attention, or judgment.

But it does allow more people to attempt the translation of an idea into form. Things we’ve knocked around in our melons for years, waiting for the right skills, money, collaborators, permission, or opportunity, may finally find a home.

And it is sad to see that possibility shunned by people perched above the wall of creativity.

I’m now seeing people deliver public mea culpas for accidentally liking something made with AI assistance. The work resonated with them. It made them laugh, or ache, or remember something. Then they discovered which tools carried it there and felt obliged to retract the experience.

The creation becomes a Trojan horse. How dare someone use tools I don’t approve of to convey a feeling that reached me? They shouldn’t be allowed. Only approved avenues from here on out.

The apparatus feeds, but it must also have the willing participation of those it feeds upon.

For decades, the established creative apparatus has decided which ideas receive money, distribution, legitimacy, and attention. If people can make more without its help, its food source may become increasingly monocultured: comic book movies, superheroes, sequels, proven intellectual property. It’s all becoming gross, and increasingly obviously so.

But AI is not innocent of the apparatus. These tools belong to enormous companies. They depend upon proprietary models, rented infrastructure, opaque rules, and vast collections of human work. The old gatekeepers may lose power only for new gatekeepers to acquire it.

The democratization may be rented.

That contradiction is not a reason to abandon the conversation. It is the conversation. AI can be a tool of corporate extraction and a creative prosthesis. It can threaten livelihoods and expand expression. It can flatten culture into an averaged sludge and give form to ideas that would otherwise never escape someone’s head.

We should be capable of holding more than one of these truths at a time.

So what is AI truly taking from you?

Is it the meal ticket? The recognition? The years spent learning a difficult process? The belief that creative ability distinguishes you from everyone else? Is it synthetic competition in the marketplace, or the collapse of an identity built around exclusive access to expression?

Perhaps the deeper argument concerns the allowable levels of abstraction between ideation and creation.

How many steps can be removed before authorship becomes illegitimate? Which steps count as expression, and which are merely execution? Which interventions are tools, which are collaborations, and which are fraud? Who gets to draw that line? Who benefits from placing it exactly where it is?

The argument is not whether mediation is allowed. Art has always been mediated. The argument is which hidden labors we still require as proof that someone deserves to speak.

Maybe the scandal is not that a machine helped make the thing. Maybe it is that the audience can no longer reliably infer which suffering, training, access, or ability lies behind it.

Are we ready for that conversation?

If a feeling reached you before you knew how it was made, what exactly changed when you learned which tools carried it there?
